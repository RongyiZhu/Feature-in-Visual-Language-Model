\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Identifying a visual concept inside a vision--language model (VLM) requires
connecting an observable property of an image to the hidden states that
represent it. This connection is difficult because visual tokens have no
discrete identities drawn from a fixed vocabulary. A token can encode
object category, appearance, position, and scene context, and its
representation changes as it is processed by the language
decoder~\cite{NeoEtAl2025}. Even when an object's location in the image is
known, that location need not identify all the tokens that carry its
category information. An interpretability method must therefore address
both how a concept is defined and how its support in the representation is
determined.

Existing approaches provide several ways to connect representations to
human concepts. Concept activation vectors and representation reading
estimate directions from examples or contrasting
inputs~\cite{KimEtAl2018,ZouEtAl2023}. Other methods describe visual
neurons, embedding components, or token representations through text
embeddings and vocabulary projections~\cite{OikarinenWeng2023,
GandelsmanEtAl2024,NeoEtAl2025}, while vision sparse autoencoders can be
evaluated through the similarity of their activating
images~\cite{PachEtAl2025}. These approaches motivate a complementary
question: can we define a category through a controlled change to visual
content and recover a transferable direction without specifying in advance
which tokens should represent that change?

We introduce a framework built around matched visual interventions.
For a target category and a sampled competitor, we paste their object
cutouts separately into the same base image, at a common requested center and target
bounding-box area. Holding the scene and compositing procedure fixed reduces variation
unrelated to the category comparison. The intervention establishes what
changed in the input, while the model's response determines how to
aggregate hidden states. Our central design principle is that knowledge of
the intervention's pixel support does not imply knowledge of its latent
support.

The framework separates support estimation from direction estimation.
First, we measure each visual token's change from the base under both
insertions and normalize these response magnitudes into continuous weights.
The same weights are then applied to the direct difference between the two
composites. Every visual token is eligible, allowing information outside
the pasted region to contribute. Averaging these contrasts over observed competitors produces one
direction per category and layer.
Under a shared-nuisance model with competitor-independent expected gain
and zero-mean weighted residuals, the expected average aligns with the
category's deviation from the menu mean when competitor coverage is
uniform. Unequal frequencies change the reference against which a
category is contrasted. These assumptions explain the
estimator's category-relative interpretation; the estimator itself is
defined directly by the intervention responses.

We assess the resulting directions on disjoint natural images. Each token
is scored by its cosine alignment with a category direction, and the
highest-scoring fraction of tokens is averaged into an image-level presence
score. This readout requires neither object locations nor spatial
annotations. Category annotations and masks are used to construct the
interventions, while image-level labels are used to evaluate the frozen
natural-image scores with ROC-AUC. Random directions passed through the
same readout provide a reference for interpreting the measured association.
Separating discovery from evaluation tests whether information extracted
from controlled composites transfers to unedited images.

The framework measures category information accessible through
intervention-derived directions. Its token weights describe responses to
an input change, and its presence scores assess transfer; neither alone
establishes that the direction causally controls model outputs. This scope
makes the method a concrete tool for studying visual representations across
layers and models without fitting a classifier on natural-image labels or
naming the extracted directions through text.

Our contributions are:
\begin{mitemize}
\item A visual intervention framework that estimates token support from
    representation changes, without using the paste mask to select hidden
    states.
\item A category-direction estimator based on symmetric response weighting
    and category contrast averaging, with an explicit account of its
    category-relative interpretation.
\item A natural-image transfer protocol using token-wise cosine alignment,
    top-fraction pooling, and presence ROC-AUC, with random-direction
    controls and separate discovery and evaluation data.
\item Controlled component ablations showing gains from paired subtraction
    and broader competitor coverage at a fixed discovery budget, with
    explicit checks on frequency weighting and area composition.
\end{mitemize}
